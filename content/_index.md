      text: |-
        Hernan Huwyler is an AI governance and enterprise risk leader who helps organizations turn complex AI opportunities and risks into practical decisions, controls, and measurable outcomes. He brings more than two decades of global experience across multinational companies and corporate environments, with work spanning technology transformation, compliance, quantitative risk, internal audit, cybersecurity, predictive analytics, and applied artificial intelligence.

        Hernan serves as an Academic Director and Executive Professor at IE Business School and IE Law School. He teaches and directs programs in corporate compliance, AI governance, predictive analytics, risk management, internal control, and related disciplines. His work connects technical systems, business responsibilities, and regulatory expectations so that governance can guide real decisions instead of remaining a policy document on a shelf.

        His professional focus sits at the intersection of three disciplines: artificial intelligence, quantitative risk modeling, and regulatory engineering. He helps leadership teams translate frameworks such as the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894, and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework into clear ownership, decision rights, lifecycle controls, monitoring practices, and evidence that can be reviewed and tested.

        The goal is practical: help organizations adopt useful AI while making accountability, risk, security, compliance, and business value easier to understand and manage.

  - block: markdown
    id: practical-work
    content:
      title: What AI Governance Looks Like in Practice
      text: |-
        Effective AI governance connects strategy with the day-to-day work of business, legal, risk, security, data, and engineering teams. It should help people answer straightforward questions: What is the system meant to do? Who is accountable for it? What could go wrong? Which controls matter? What evidence shows that the controls are working? When should the system be changed, restricted, or stopped?

        Hernan's work helps organizations build and improve practical capabilities such as:

        - Defining AI governance operating models, accountable owners, decision rights, and escalation paths.
        - Creating AI inventories and risk-classification processes that teams can keep current.
        - Translating laws, standards, and internal risk appetite into usable requirements, controls, and evidence.
        - Assessing model, data, privacy, cybersecurity, operational, third-party, and business risks.
        - Governing generative AI, retrieval-augmented generation, foundation models, and AI agents across their lifecycle.
        - Improving AI procurement, vendor due diligence, data-use terms, and technology supply-chain oversight.
        - Establishing human oversight, testing, monitoring thresholds, incident response, and rollback procedures.
        - Connecting AI governance to existing enterprise risk, compliance, internal audit, and security processes.
        - Evaluating business cases and measuring AI value alongside cost, uncertainty, and risk.

        This is not about adding paperwork for its own sake. A useful control makes a decision safer or clearer, assigns responsibility to the people who can act, and produces evidence that can be checked later.

  - block: markdown
    id: audience-and-approach
    content:
      title: Who This Work Is For—and How It Helps
      text: |-
        This work is relevant to Chief AI Officers, AI governance leaders, GRC and enterprise-risk teams, model-risk specialists, auditors, compliance and privacy professionals, security leaders, AI architects, engineers, procurement teams, business sponsors, and boards. Each group sees a different part of the problem. The operating model has to connect those perspectives without making useful AI impossible to deploy.

        Hernan's approach is to start with the business decision, then make the system, risk, authority, and evidence requirements explicit. That means understanding the intended outcome; identifying affected people, processes, data, and dependencies; choosing controls that match the risk; assigning an owner; and deciding how performance and risk will be monitored after launch. When something changes, the organization should know who reviews it and what evidence to retain.

        The result should be understandable to the people doing the work and credible to the people providing oversight. AI governance should be rigorous enough to support accountability, flexible enough to accommodate different use cases, and practical enough to improve decisions rather than delay them by default.
